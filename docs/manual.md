## What this is, in plain language

Start here if the words FHIR and OMOP mean nothing to you.

Hospitals store patient records in systems that can hand out one patient's data at a time, on request, to an app that needs it. The format they use for that is called **FHIR**. It is excellent at its job: *"give me everything about this one person, now."*

Researchers need the opposite. They want to ask *"how many people like this, across every hospital in the country, and what happened to them?"* FHIR cannot answer that without downloading every patient one at a time and writing bespoke code to compare them. So the research community built a second format, **OMOP CDM**, designed only for that kind of question.

The two formats hold the same facts in completely different shapes. Somebody has to write the converter.

This pipeline is that converter. It reads 1,180 patients' worth of FHIR files and writes an OMOP database you can ask population questions of in ordinary SQL.

### What it actually did

**1,180** — patients read

**414K** — FHIR resources in

**396K** — CDM rows out

**36/36** — quality checks pass

### The data is synthetic

Every patient here is generated by [Synthea](https://synthetichealth.github.io/synthea-sample-data/), a simulator that produces realistic but entirely fictional medical histories. No real person's data is involved anywhere in this project. That matters for two reasons: nothing here carries privacy obligations, and nothing here should be mistaken for a finding about real people.

### Why it was built

As a learning exercise, and it works because the two formats disagree constantly. Every disagreement forces a decision you have to make consciously — FHIR says "this is a diagnosis", OMOP says "that concept belongs in the observations table". Reading a specification lets you nod past those. Writing the converter does not.

## The vocabulary — the part that does the work

Understand this and the rest of the system makes sense.

Consider one medical idea: *high blood pressure with no identifiable cause*. Different systems write it down differently:

one idea, many codes

    SNOMED CT   59621000    "Essential hypertension"
    ICD-10      I10         "Essential (primary) hypertension"
    ICD-9-CM    401.9       "Unspecified essential hypertension"
    a hospital  HTN-1       "HTN, essential"

If you store whatever code arrived, you cannot count patients across hospitals. OMOP solves this by giving the *idea itself* its own number, independent of any coding system. Essential hypertension is **`320128`**. Every one of those source codes points at it.

That number is called a **concept_id**, and it is the single most important thing in OMOP. Every clinical fact in the database is stored as one.

### The vocabulary file

The translation table is enormous and is maintained by the OHDSI community, not by you. This pipeline uses a copy downloaded from [Athena](https://athena.ohdsi.org/):

| Table                 | What it holds                                     | Rows       |
|-----------------------|---------------------------------------------------|------------|
| concept               | The dictionary. Every medical idea, one row each. | 4,254,142  |
| concept_relationship | Edges between concepts, including `Maps to`       | 35,221,992 |
| concept_ancestor     | The hierarchy, precomputed at every depth         | 38,996,579 |
| concept_synonym      | Alternative names for searching                   | 2,449,429  |

Downloading that is downloading years of other people's terminology work. It is the reason OMOP is worth the trouble.

### Two mechanisms people confuse

**Maps to — equivalence**

"This code means the same thing as that standard concept." Handles deprecated codes and cross-vocabulary translation. Runs at **load time**, normalising whatever arrives.

Example: SNOMED retired `Prediabetes` in 2002. It still arrives in data. `Maps to` redirects it to `Impaired glucose tolerance`, which is current.

**concept_ancestor — subsumption**

"This is a *kind of* that." Pre-eclampsia is not the same as hypertensive disorder — it is a specific form of it. Runs at **analysis time**, letting you ask for a whole family of concepts at once.

Asking for everything beneath "Hypertensive disorder" returns 139 concepts, including forms you would not have thought to list.

### Standard and non-standard concepts

Because concepts are imported from many source vocabularies, OMOP ends up holding several that mean roughly the same thing. It nominates exactly one as **standard**; everything else exists only to be translated *from*.

**The failure this causes**

Storing a non-standard concept raises no error. The row inserts, foreign keys resolve, checks pass. But every cohort query in the OHDSI world searches for standard concepts, so those patients are not *rejected* — they are **invisible**. You see a number that is too small and nothing tells you why.

This happened twice during construction. Both times it was caught by a check, not by reading the specification.

## How the ETL runs: nine passes

The order is forced by the data, not chosen for convenience.

**ETL** stands for Extract, Transform, Load — read from the source, reshape, write to the target. The reason this one runs in passes rather than straight through is that clinical records point at each other.

A diagnosis says "this belongs to patient `urn:uuid:5cbc121b…`". To write it, you need that patient's row to already exist and to know what number you gave them. So people must be loaded before diagnoses. The same applies to visits, and to the doctors and hospitals that everything else references.

**Each pass reads every file before the next begins**

This matters more than it sounds. An earlier version nested all the passes inside one loop over files, which quietly assumes **each file is self-contained**.

That held for Synthea, where every file is one patient's complete bundle, so a `Patient` is always read before the `Encounter` referencing it. A bulk export splits resources *by type*, so `Condition.ndjson` is processed before `Patient.ndjson` exists — and every clinical row was rejected while the run still reported success.

pass 0

#### Doctors, hospitals, addresses

Practitioner → `provider`, Organization → `care_site`, their addresses → `location`. **Deduplicated across the whole corpus**: the same doctor appears in a file for every patient they treated, so 2,980 records become 1,036 rows.

pass 1

#### People

Patient → `person`. Builds the index from FHIR patient id to the `person_id` this pipeline assigns.

pass 2

#### Visits

Encounter → `visit_occurrence`. Needs pass 1 (a visit belongs to a patient) and pass 0 (it happened at a hospital, with a doctor). Builds the visit index.

pass 3a–3h

#### Everything clinical

Conditions, observations, procedures, prescriptions, administrations, vaccines, allergies, devices. Each needs both indexes.

pass 4

#### Derived tables

`observation_period`, `drug_era`, `condition_era`. Computed from every event already loaded, so they must come last.

### The rule that governs every mapping

This is the single most counterintuitive thing in the system, and it appears in five different modules:

The destination table is decided by the **domain of the concept**, not by the type of the FHIR resource that carried it.

A FHIR `Condition` does not always become a `condition_occurrence`. "Normal pregnancy" arrives as a Condition and is an *Observation* in OMOP. A FHIR `Procedure` often is not a procedure either:

| Arrived as | Resources | Went to               | Rows   |
|------------|-----------|-----------------------|--------|
| Procedure  | 36,451    | procedure_occurrence | 28,401 |
|            |           | measurement           | 7,528  |
|            |           | observation           | 522    |
| Condition  | 8,766     | condition_occurrence | 8,583  |
|            |           | observation           | 183    |

Getting this wrong produces no error. The rows insert, every key resolves, and prevalence queries return a wrong denominator forever.

tracing a row back to its source

    SELECT p.person_id, p.person_source_value, e.source_file, e.source_resource
    FROM   person p
    JOIN   _etl_provenance e
      ON   e.cdm_table = 'person' AND e.cdm_pk = p.person_id
    LIMIT  3;

### The 21 empty tables

The CDM defines about 40 tables. This pipeline fills 19 and creates the rest empty — `death`, `note`, `specimen`, `cost`, `payer_plan_period` and so on. That is deliberate: OHDSI tools treat a missing table as an error, but an empty one as a legitimate answer. "This site records no specimens" is a real and common fact about a dataset.

## The derived tables

Three tables with no FHIR counterpart, and the reasoning behind each.

### observation_period — the denominator

A rate is one number divided by another. For "what fraction of people have hypertension": the top is people with the diagnosis, the bottom is people who *could* have been diagnosed.

The top is easy to count. The bottom is a judgement, and it is what this table answers. Someone who appears in your data for a single day was never realistically going to be diagnosed with anything — counting them in the denominator makes every rate look too low.

**Nothing in FHIR says when a person was observable.** It has to be derived. This pipeline uses first-to-last recorded event across every domain table, which is the usual convention for EHR-derived data and is a *floor*, not a truth: it cannot see that a patient moved away three years before their last visit.

The CDM lets you record which method you used, and this one says so honestly — `period_type_concept_id = 32882`, "Standard algorithm from EHR".

### drug_era — treatment episodes

Twelve monthly prescriptions of the same drug are twelve rows in `drug_exposure`. Clinically they are *one twelve-month course of treatment*. Counting rows tells you about prescribing; counting eras tells you about **exposure** — and nearly every question worth asking is about exposure.

Two mechanisms build them:

-   **A 30-day persistence window.** Exposures closer together than that are one era; a longer gap starts a new one. It tolerates a late refill without merging courses of treatment years apart.
-   **Ingredient rollup.** Drugs arrive as "Lisinopril 10 MG Tablet", branded packs, quantified forms. Eras are built at the *ingredient* level via `concept_ancestor`, so every form of one drug collapses into a single era.

What that reveals in this data:

exposures collapsing into eras

    ingredient      exposures   eras   avg era days
    cisplatin             880    473             24   ← chemo cycling
    paclitaxel            844    475             22
    simvastatin         2,363  2,360              0   ← see below

    longest single episode:
    epoetin alfa    1,214 scripts across 10 years in one era

Chemotherapy collapses nearly 2:1 because those administrations genuinely cluster within days. The epoetin alfa case is a dialysis patient on continuous treatment — 1,214 unremarkable rows in `drug_exposure`, one decade-long course as an era.

**Why simvastatin does not collapse**

Synthea supplies no dispense duration, so every `drug_exposure` has an end date equal to its start. A statin prescribed annually is a point event 365 days from the next one — well outside the 30-day window — so it correctly forms separate eras.

With real dispensing data carrying `days_supply`, simvastatin would form multi-year eras. That one missing field is the difference between eras that describe treatment and eras that just re-describe prescriptions.

### condition_era

The same collapsing applied to diagnoses, with no rollup — there is no diagnosis equivalent of a drug ingredient, so it uses the concept directly.

### Querying the result directly

any DuckDB client

    duckdb -readonly data/omop/omop.duckdb

**One operational rule**

DuckDB takes an **exclusive write lock**. Only one process can write to a database file at a time — a second ETL run started while the first is going will fail with a lock error. Read-only connections are unlimited, so querying while a load runs is fine.

## Reading the output

The ETL prints four blocks. Here is what each means.

### Three input shapes, one reader

The ETL accepts a Bundle (a Synthea file or a search result), a bare single resource, or newline-delimited JSON (what Bulk Data returns, since a population export is far too large for one JSON document). All three are flattened to a list of resources before any mapping happens, so nothing downstream needs to know where the data came from.

### Block 1 — what came in and what went out

    FHIR resources read:
      Condition                 8766
      Observation             259929
      ...
    CDM rows written:
      condition_occurrence      8583
      measurement             253867
      ...
    rejected: 0

Counts will not match between the two lists, and should not. Resources get routed to different tables by domain, some are excluded by status, and several FHIR types feed one CDM table. **`rejected: 0` is the number to watch** — a rejection means a resource could not be loaded at all, and each one is listed with a reason.

### Block 2 — mapping issues, by frequency

      14102  no dispense duration in source; end_date set to start_date
       5561  non-standard 3030354 mapped to standard 46236952
        388  non-standard 40316773 mapped to standard 37018196
        300  concept 40213291 is domain 'Drug'; no target table, concept set to 0

These are **not errors**. They are decisions the pipeline made and is telling you about. The second and third lines are the `Maps to` machinery working — deprecated codes being redirected. The fourth is the domain rule refusing to put a concept in a table where it does not belong.

### Block 3 — data quality checks

      [     PASS] conformance  condition_concept_in_condition_domain
      [     PASS] plausibility events_within_observation_period
      [FAIL (102)] conformance  observation_concept_in_observation_domain

36 checks in three categories:

-   **Conformance** — does the data obey the CDM's structural rules? Answerable without knowing anything about the source.
-   **Completeness** — is a required value present, and how much did not map?
-   **Plausibility** — could this be true of a real person? Dates in order, events after birth, eras not overlapping.

### Block 4 — unmapped rates

      condition_occurrence.condition_concept_id     109/8583   (1.3% unmapped)
      measurement.measurement_concept_id              0/253867 (0.0% unmapped)
      drug_exposure.drug_concept_id                  22/30041  (0.1% unmapped)
      person.race_concept_id                       1180/1180   (100.0% unmapped)

This is the single most useful number in the whole report, and the reason it is a headline rather than a footnote: **a pipeline can pass every structural check while mapping almost nothing.** Only this figure reveals it.

"Unmapped" means the concept resolved to `0` — OMOP's reserved value for "no matching concept". That is the correct, specification-compliant way to say a code did not translate. Guessing a plausible concept instead is how a dataset becomes quietly and permanently wrong.

The race and ethnicity figure of 100% is a *decision*, explained in section 13.

## Quality checking

Two suites, doing different jobs.

### The built-in suite — 36 checks

Runs automatically at the end of every ETL. Fast, specific to the decisions this pipeline makes, and each check exists because something could plausibly go wrong there. Written in `src/fhir_omop/dq.py`, and each returns a count of violations so zero always means pass.

### The OHDSI Data Quality Dashboard — 2,533 checks

The community-standard suite, generated from about twenty check types applied across every table, field and concept the CDM defines. Same three categories, vastly more coverage.

**2,533** — checks run

**2,530** — pass

**2** — fail — both explained

**1** — errors — upstream bug

### What "fail" means here — it is not what you think

Every DQD check has a **threshold**: the percentage of violating rows it tolerates. A check fails when violations exceed that. It is not "this is broken", it is "this exceeds the tolerance the community judged reasonable".

That distinction matters enormously in practice. When DQD was first run against this database it reported nine failures. **Only one was a defect.** Six were a threshold that does not apply to this dataset — DQD flags clinical dates before 1950 as implausible, and Synthea generates complete lifetimes, so patients born in the 1910s legitimately have pre-1950 records. Two were units genuinely absent from OMOP's vocabulary.

**The skill this teaches**

Reading a check suite *critically* is the actual skill. Chasing all nine failures to green would have meant fabricating units and truncating real patient history.

But ignoring a red check and overriding it with a recorded reason look identical in the dashboard. Only one of them answers "why is this failing?" when someone opens it in six months. That is what `scripts/make_thresholds.R` is for — it regenerates the override from DQD's own defaults, changes exactly one thing, and writes the reason into the file.

## Cohorts: asking it questions

The thing the whole pipeline exists to enable.

A **cohort** is the atom of observational research: the set of people meeting some criteria, over some period. Every study in this field is cohorts compared against cohorts.

Three are defined in `scripts/build_cohorts.R`:

| Cohort               | Definition                                                      | People |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|--------|
| hypertension_adults | Adults, first hypertensive disorder, 365 days prior observation | 173    |
| t2dm_any            | First type 2 diabetes diagnosis, 365 days prior observation     | 95     |
| hctz_new_users     | First exposure to hydrochlorothiazide                           | 138    |

### Why R and not ATLAS

ATLAS is the web interface most OHDSI work is done through. Its backend does not support DuckDB — only PostgreSQL, SQL Server, Oracle, Redshift and Snowflake — so running it would mean migrating everything to Postgres and standing up three Docker containers.

**Capr drives the same engine ATLAS does** (`circe-be`) and emits the same cohort JSON. CirceR compiles it with the same compiler. The only difference is that here the JSON and the generated SQL are visible files you can read, instead of being hidden behind a UI.

a definition, in R

    hypertension <- cs(descendants(316866), name = "Hypertensive disorder")

    hypertension_adults <- cohort(
      entry = entry(
        conditionOccurrence(hypertension, age(gte(18))),
        observationWindow = continuousObservation(priorDays = 365),
        primaryCriteriaLimit = "First"
      ),
      exit = exit(endStrategy = observationExit())
    )

That 1.3 KB definition compiles to 13 KB of SQL. The top of the generated file is a temp table doing the `concept_ancestor` join that turns one concept into all 139 of its descendants.

### What generating them revealed

**The age criterion excludes nobody.** Every hypertensive patient in this data is already an adult at first diagnosis. The entire reduction from 341 people with hypertension to 173 in the cohort is the *365-day prior-observation requirement* — which is the criterion that makes this an incident cohort rather than a prevalent one.

**The same words, two different cohorts**

A hand-written version of this query returns **171**. Capr returns **173**. Neither is wrong.

"First diagnosis with 365 days of prior observation" has two readings. Take the earliest diagnosis and then test whether it qualifies → 171. Keep only diagnoses that qualify and then take the earliest → 173.

Two people have an early diagnosis without enough prior observation *and* a later qualifying one. Under one reading they are excluded; under the other they are included with a later index date — which shifts every follow-up window and every outcome look-back for them.

This is the real argument for using the standard engine. Not that it is faster, but that it commits to one interpretation — the same one every other OHDSI study uses. Your 173 is comparable to someone else's 173.

## Extending it

Adding a new FHIR resource type, step by step.

Every mapping module has the same shape, so adding one is mechanical. Roughly 150 lines plus tests.

1.  **Look at the actual resource first.** Not the specification — a real example from your data. Check which field names it uses for patient and encounter, which status values appear, and which date fields are present. Every resource type differs.
2.  **Write the module** in `src/fhir_omop/mappings/`. Use `references.resolve()` for every reference and `vocab.resolve_to_standard()` for every code — never reimplement either.
3.  **Guard the domain.** Check the resolved concept's `domain_id` and route accordingly. If there is no table for that domain, keep the fact and set the concept to `0`.
4.  **Verify any concept id you hardcode** against the loaded vocabulary before using it. Three non-standard type concepts shipped in this project because they were taken from memory rather than checked.
5.  **Wire it into `etl.py`** as a new pass 3 stage, and add the insert statement to `INSERT_SQL`.
6.  **Add quality checks** to `dq.py`: a person foreign key, a domain check, and a date-order check at minimum.
7.  **Write the tests before committing**, not after. Four modules once shipped untested here and a coverage audit had to catch them.

### The traps, collected

These cost real debugging time and will cost you the same:

| Resource                          | Patient field | Encounter field |
|-----------------------------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Condition, Observation, Procedure | .subject      | .encounter      |
| Immunization                      | **.patient**  | .encounter      |
| AllergyIntolerance, Device        | **.patient**  | —               |
| MedicationAdministration          | .subject      | **.context**    |

FHIR has no single field name for "the patient this is about". Assuming otherwise silently orphans every row of that type — and orphans are rejected, so you get a clean run with an empty result and no error.

## Troubleshooting

Every failure actually hit during construction.

### "Could not set lock on file … Conflicting lock is held"

Two processes are trying to write the same DuckDB file. Find and stop the other one:

    ps aux | grep run_etl
    kill <pid>
    rm -f data/omop/*.wal   # if a stale write-ahead log remains

### The dashboard opens but every panel is empty

You gave `viewDqDashboard` a relative path. It stores the path, then Shiny changes the working directory before reading it, so the file is looked for inside the R package. The JSON parse fails and the app renders its shell with no error at all. Use an absolute path.

### Everything is 100% unmapped

Almost always the vocabulary. Either it was not passed (`--vocab`), or it does not contain the vocabulary that codes are arriving in. Check what is actually loaded:

    duckdb -readonly data/omop/vocab.duckdb \
      -c "SELECT vocabulary_id, count(*) FROM base.concept GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 2 DESC;"

This exact situation produced 15,013 unmapped immunizations, because CVX had not been selected in the Athena download.

### A whole resource type produces zero rows

The reference field is wrong — see the table in section 11. Rejections are reported, so check the rejected list for "unresolved subject".

### A DQD check errors rather than fails

Errors are plumbing, not findings. One check fails to compile here: its SQL uses `DATE + INTERVAL`, an operation provided by DuckDB's ICU extension, which DatabaseConnector installs but never loads. One check in 2,533, on a metadata field, and an upstream issue rather than a data problem.

### A concept id you hardcoded does not work

Check whether it is standard. Non-standard concepts produce no error and no match:

    SELECT concept_id, concept_name, standard_concept, invalid_reason
    FROM   concept WHERE concept_id = 38000177;

    -- standard_concept = NULL means it will never match a cohort definition

## Known limits

Deliberate decisions, not defects. Recorded so nobody mistakes them for either.

### Race and ethnicity are 100% unmapped, by decision

CDM 5.4 marks both as required fields. But the race extension Synthea emits is a *US* construct, and **Australia's FHIR profile has no race extension at all** — Australian data records Indigenous status under a different model that does not map onto OMOP's race/ethnicity pair.

Setting both to `0` is the honest answer. Inventing categories would produce breakdowns that describe the ETL rather than the population.

### Drug exposures have no duration

Synthea supplies no dispense duration, so `drug_exposure_end_date` equals its start. This understates exposure and is why chronic medications form no long eras. Real dispensing data with `days_supply` would change this substantially.

### Vaccines produce no drug eras

CVX vaccine concepts are outside the RxNorm ingredient hierarchy, so 15,013 immunizations roll up to nothing. That is the standard OHDSI definition, not an omission — but it means `drug_era` covers a strict subset of `drug_exposure` and their counts will never reconcile.

### observation_period is inferred, not observed

Derived from first-to-last event. A floor, not a truth. Claims data supports a better derivation because enrolment spans are recorded explicitly.

### Some units cannot be mapped

OMOP's UCUM vocabulary is about 1,130 concepts and does not include enzyme units (`U/L`) or annotation units (`{score}`). Roughly 29,800 measurements carry a value with no resolvable unit. Not fixable without inventing data.

### What is not mapped at all

-   **Claim and ExplanationOfBenefit** — billing data, mapping to `cost` and `payer_plan_period`. Health economics, and a project of its own.
-   **DiagnosticReport** — a grouping resource whose member observations are already mapped. Largely redundant in OMOP, which has no equivalent table.
-   **CarePlan, CareTeam, Goal, ImagingStudy** — no clean home in CDM 5.4 core.

One theme runs through every defect this project hit, and it is worth carrying forward: **almost none of them raised an error.** The rows inserted, the foreign keys resolved, the tests passed, and the numbers were quietly wrong. That is why the check suites exist, why unmapped rates are a headline number, and why testing against a realistically-sized vocabulary matters more than testing against a convenient one.
